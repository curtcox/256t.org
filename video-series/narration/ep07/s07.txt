Now let's meet the neighbors, because seeing where two fifty-six tee sits among them is the best way to understand it.

At one extreme are data U-R-Is. They put the entire content directly inside the link. That's exactly two fifty-six tee's inline case, except that data U-R-Is do it for content of any size, so they can get huge.

Subresource Integrity is the web's way of saying "load this script from this location, but refuse it unless its hash matches this." It's verification bolted onto an ordinary address. It solves the content-drift problem, but if the location disappears, you're stuck, because the fingerprint isn't used to find anything.

Git names every file's contents by a hash. And in a nice bit of convergent design, the thing Git hashes starts with the word "blob" and the file's length, before the content. Length plus fingerprint. Git has used SHA one, which is part of why the SHA one collisions we saw caused real concern, and it has been moving toward SHA two-fifty-six.

There's even an internet standard for names like these, called "Naming Things with Hashes." Its links start with the letters n i, and they label which hash algorithm was used. And I P F S goes furthest: self-describing names that label their hash algorithm and content type, chunked trees for large files, and a whole peer-to-peer network for finding things.

Two fifty-six tee sits in a deliberately small spot on this map. Smaller than I P F S, more findable than Subresource Integrity, and more compact than a data URI. It's just a name, at most ninety-four characters, that fits in a single segment of a URL, and that anyone can compute.
