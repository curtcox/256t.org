Last time we found that accidental collisions are so unlikely you can stop thinking about them. But an adversary is not an accident. An adversary gets to choose their inputs. They can study the algorithm, rent warehouses of computers, and wait.

The two fifty-six tee spec has a line about this that I really like. "It is easier to just lie than engineer a collision." That one sentence contains the whole structure of this episode, because there are really two kinds of adversary. There's the liar, who simply hands you the wrong bytes. And there's the forger, who tries to make wrong bytes that genuinely have the right name. Let's take them in order of how much effort they're willing to spend.

The liar's strategy is to serve you something other than what you asked for. Maybe it's malware instead of the installer. Maybe it's an edited version of a document. It costs them nothing, and if you don't check, it works every time.

But we know how to check. The name is computed from the content, so when the bytes arrive, you compute their C-I-D yourself and compare. If the liar sent anything other than the real content, the C-I-D won't match, and you throw it away.

This defense is absolute in a way that's unusual in security. It doesn't depend on how clever the liar is, or how many computers they have. Any bytes other than the right ones produce a different C-I-D, unless the liar has found a genuine collision. And that's the forger's job, not the liar's. So verification takes the cheapest attack there is, and turns it into the most expensive one.

The forger wants something harder: different bytes that really do have the right name. Remember the three games from episode three? The forger is playing one of the last two, and which one depends on a question that's easy to skip past. Who made the original?

Suppose the original was made by someone else. It's your file, or a document published by somebody honest. Then the forger has to find a second preimage, different content with the same C-I-D as content they had no part in creating. With SHA five-twelve, the generic cost of that is around two to the five hundred twelve tries. That's not happening.

But now suppose the forger made the original. Then they get to play the easier game. They don't need to hit your target. They just need any two documents of their own design with matching names. One harmless, one harmful. That's a collision, and thanks to the birthday effect, the generic cost drops to about two to the two hundred fifty-six.

Here's why that matters. Picture it as a story. The forger sends you a contract. You read it carefully, you approve it, and you record its C-I-D. Later, you fetch that C-I-D, from somewhere the forger has influence, and you get the other twin. You verify it. And the check passes. Because, as far as two fifty-six tee is concerned, both twins genuinely have that name.

So verification defeats the liar completely, but it can't defeat a true collision. That's the honest crack in "a C-I-D uniquely determines its content." The question is how wide the crack is.

So, is two to the two hundred fifty-six a lot? Let's use the biggest pile of hashing hardware humanity has ever built: the Bitcoin network. As of late 2025 it was doing around a zettahash per second, about ten to the twenty-one hashes every second, which is roughly two to the seventy. It computes a different hash, SHA two-fifty-six, but it gives us a sense of scale for "every specialized hashing machine on Earth."

Point all of that at a SHA five-twelve collision by brute force. Two to the two hundred fifty-six, divided by two to the seventy per second, is two to the one hundred eighty-six seconds. That's around three times ten to the forty-eight years. The universe is about fourteen billion years old. You'd need it to happen again roughly ten to the thirty-eight times over.

And quantum computers? The best known quantum algorithms do lower the exponents for these problems. But even under optimistic assumptions about future hardware, the work for a SHA five-twelve collision stays at numbers like two to the hundred and seventy, which has more than fifty digits. Brute force is simply not how anyone will forge a two fifty-six tee name.

Here's the thing, though. Real hash functions have been broken. Just never by brute force. Let's look at how it actually happened.

M D five was published in 1992, with a hundred-and-twenty-eight bit output. In 2004, a team led by Xiaoyun Wang showed how to find M D five collisions quickly, not by trying more inputs, but by exploiting structure in how M D five mixes its bits. Within a few years, researchers used M D five collisions to forge a certificate authority certificate. And in 2012, a piece of espionage malware called Flame was found using a previously unknown M D five collision technique to forge a Microsoft code-signing certificate, so that it could spread disguised as a legitimate software update. That's the twins scenario, exactly, in the wild.

SHA one, with a hundred-and-sixty bit output, followed a similar arc, just more slowly. A theoretical shortcut was published in 2005. In 2017, researchers from Google and C W I Amsterdam produced two different PDF files with the same SHA one hash, at a cost of around two to the sixty-three computations, on the order of a hundred G P U-years. And by 2020, a stronger form called a chosen-prefix collision, where both documents can say whatever the attacker wants, plus a bit of gibberish, was estimated to cost in the tens of thousands of dollars.

Three lessons. First, these functions died from shortcuts, cleverness about their internal structure, not raw computing power. Second, there were years of warning between the first cracks and practical attacks. And third, their outputs were small. M D five's birthday bound was only two to the sixty-four, and SHA one's two to the eighty. They didn't have much margin to lose.

So where does SHA five-twelve stand? It's been around for over two decades, it's one of the most studied functions in cryptography, and no collision for it has ever been found.

Researchers do probe it, the way they probed M D five and SHA one. They attack weakened versions with fewer rounds. The best practical published collisions reach twenty-seven of its eighty steps. In an even more favorable setting, where the attacker also gets to choose the internal starting state, which a real attacker can't, they reach thirty-nine steps. That's a meaningful distance from the full eighty. And its birthday bound starts at two to the two hundred fifty-six, so even a shortcut that knocked off a huge chunk of that would leave an astronomical number behind.

Could that change someday? In principle. But if history is a guide, we'd see the cracks coming years ahead.

Does two fifty-six tee's structure add anything here? Partly. Inline C-I-Ds can't be forged at all, since the content is the name. The length prefix, honestly, doesn't help much against a forger: someone building twins just makes them the same length. It helps a lot against a different kind of misbehavior, which we'll see next episode. For everything else, a two fifty-six tee name is exactly as strong as SHA five-twelve's collision resistance.

Let's put it together. For an adversary to cause a real problem collision, three things have to line up. They have to find a genuine SHA five-twelve collision, which no one has ever done. They have to be the author of content you're relying on, so they can plant one twin. And they have to be in a position to hand you the other. Without the first, they're just a liar, and verification catches them every time.

And so the spec's advice comes down to two habits. First: always verify. It's one hash computation, and it reduces every cheap attack to the expensive one. Second: reduce adversaries. Be thoughtful about trusting content whose author would benefit from swapping it, and about where you fetch from. If nobody is putting malicious content where you might accept it, you're back to accidents only, and we know how that story ends.
