Here's the thing, though. Real hash functions have been broken. Just never by brute force. Let's look at how it actually happened.

M D five was published in 1992, with a hundred-and-twenty-eight bit output. In 2004, a team led by Xiaoyun Wang showed how to find M D five collisions quickly, not by trying more inputs, but by exploiting structure in how M D five mixes its bits. Within a few years, researchers used M D five collisions to forge a certificate authority certificate. And in 2012, a piece of espionage malware called Flame was found using a previously unknown M D five collision technique to forge a Microsoft code-signing certificate, so that it could spread disguised as a legitimate software update. That's the twins scenario, exactly, in the wild.

Shaw one, with a hundred-and-sixty bit output, followed a similar arc, just more slowly. A theoretical shortcut was published in 2005. In 2017, researchers from Google and C W I Amsterdam produced two different PDF files with the same Shaw one hash, at a cost of around two to the sixty-three computations, on the order of a hundred G P U-years. And by 2020, a stronger form called a chosen-prefix collision, where both documents can say whatever the attacker wants, plus a bit of gibberish, was estimated to cost in the tens of thousands of dollars.

Three lessons. First, these functions died from shortcuts, cleverness about their internal structure, not raw computing power. Second, there were years of warning between the first cracks and practical attacks. And third, their outputs were small. M D five's birthday bound was only two to the sixty-four, and Shaw one's two to the eighty. They didn't have much margin to lose.
