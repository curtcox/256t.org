Change "Hello" to "hello" and 239 of the 512 bits in its SHA-512 hash flip. Collisions must exist, by simple counting, so what exactly is a "collision-resistant" hash promising?

A hash function turns any amount of data into a fixed-size fingerprint, the same way on every computer. But there are 2^520 files of 65 bytes and only 2^512 possible SHA-512 outputs, so by the pigeonhole principle collisions are everywhere. The promise isn't that they don't exist. It's that nobody knows how to find one.

We separate the three security games, preimage, second preimage and collision, and see why the last one costs about 2^256 instead of 2^512. Then a peek inside SHA-512's rounds shows one flipped bit avalanching across the state, and the ideal coin-flipping ledger that real hashes are measured against. Finally: which of the three games 256t actually relies on, and why a CID is a name, not a lock.
