"It is easier to just lie than engineer a collision." An attacker can hand you the wrong bytes, or try to forge different bytes that genuinely have the right name. One hash computation defeats the first completely. The second has never been done against SHA-512.

We meet two adversaries. The liar serves the wrong content, and verification catches them every time, whatever their budget. The forger needs a real collision, and which game they play depends on who made the original: a second preimage at about 2^512 tries, or, if they authored both "twins", a collision at about 2^256. Even the whole Bitcoin network, around a zettahash per second as of late 2025, would need around 3 × 10^48 years.

Real hash functions do die, though, never by brute force. We trace how MD5 fell (Wang et al. in 2004, a rogue certificate authority in 2008, the Flame malware's forged Microsoft certificate in 2012) and SHA-1 (SHAttered in 2017, a chosen-prefix collision in 2020), where SHA-512 stands today, and the spec's two defenses: always verify, and reduce adversaries.
