About 38% of web pages from 2013 were gone a decade later, and half the links in U.S. Supreme Court opinions no longer lead to what the Justices cited. Every one of those links named a place. What if a name described the content instead?

This episode builds content addressing from scratch. A web address is a set of directions: go to this computer, this folder, ask for this name. That's why it breaks when the house moves (link rot) and lies when a new house goes up on the same spot (content drift). Compute the name from the bytes themselves and a surprising list of properties comes for free: any copy is as good as the original, you can check what you were handed, caches never go stale, and nothing can change underneath its name.

Then we design the recipe, try a few that fail, and meet 256t: a name of at most 94 URL-safe characters, made of the content's length plus either its SHA-512 fingerprint or, for 64 bytes or less, the content itself. We end on the honest asterisk the spec itself admits to: some different files must share a name.
